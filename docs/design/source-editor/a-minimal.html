The Six Minds of Experience

A common definition of wayfinding is that it is about getting from point A to point B. For the products you build it is richer than that: it is the sum of every cue that tells someone where they are, what is around them, and how to get back.

That definition is deliberately loose, because wayfinding is not one thing. It is a habit of the person moving through a space, and it is assembled out of whatever happens to be in front of them. A street sign, a colour, the shape of a doorway, the fact that the noisy room is behind you: all of it feeds the same running calculation. When the calculation succeeds, nobody notices it happened. When it fails, the failure is felt immediately and named precisely — I don't know where I am — which is why the craft of wayfinding is largely a craft of removing doubt rather than adding signage.

Interfaces inherit this. A person opening a document has the same three questions they have at a train station, and they ask them in the same order.

MindThe question it answers
Vision / AttentionWhat should I look at first?
WayfindingWhere am I, and how do I get where I am going?
MemoryHave I seen this before?
LanguageDoes this make sense?
Decision makingWhat should I do next?
EmotionHow do I feel about this?

The useful thing about the list is that it is ordered by how cheaply each question can be answered, and by how expensive it is to answer it late. Attention is resolved in a few hundred milliseconds and cannot be deferred. Emotion is resolved continuously and quietly, and is usually only noticed when it has already gone wrong.

Vision, Attention, and Automaticity

Attention is a spotlight with a narrow beam and a very short memory. Anything the interface expects a person to find by searching has already cost them something they cannot get back, because finding is serial and looking is parallel.

The most expensive words in an interface are the ones a person has to read twice.

Automaticity is the other half of the story. Skills that are practised enough stop consuming attention, and a person who has stopped attending is faster, calmer, and far harder to interrupt. The interface that supports automaticity is the one whose important things never move, whose labels never change meaning, and whose layout survives the arrival of new content without rearranging itself.

Applying it to a reading surface

  • One column, one measure, one place the eye can rest.
  • Landmarks at every scale: a title, a section, a paragraph, a line.
  • Nothing that has to be hunted — if a control matters, it is present or it is one labelled move away.

The last point is the one that is most often got wrong, and it is worth separating the two ideas properly. A control that is near the text costs nothing: the eye passes over it, registers it as furniture, and moves on. A control that is in the reading path interrupts the sentence, and an interrupted sentence is a sentence read twice.

Signposting the reader

Progress is the cheapest landmark there is. A reader who can see how far in they are will keep reading; a reader who cannot will go looking for a scrollbar instead, and the scrollbar will tell them something worse: that the end is a long way off and that they have no idea where they are in it.

There are two ways to answer the question, and they are not equivalent. A percentage is a number the reader has to convert into a feeling. A position — a chapter name, a section marker, a progress line that visibly belongs to a document rather than to a task — is already a feeling.

Landmarks at every scale

A long document needs landmarks at more than one scale, because a reader arrives with more than one intention. Some arrive to read it through and need the shape of the whole thing. Some arrive to check one fact and need to find a heading they can recognise. Some arrive knowing the exact phrase they want and need only a way back to where they left off.

The problem with panels

Panels are the default answer to the problem of having too much to show, and they are a good answer for a great many things. They are a poor answer for reading, for a structural reason: a panel takes its space permanently, while the need for the panel is intermittent.

A reader who checks the provenance of a source once, at the start, does not need provenance occupying a third of the screen for the rest of the session. The panel has been paid for in measure — the width of the text — and measure is the single resource that cannot be recovered by any other means.

What this leaves

Take the index out of the reading path, take the panels out of the measure, and leave the text the whole of what remains. Then use the smallest possible amount of chrome to answer the three questions a reader actually has — what is this, where am I, what can I do with it — and put everything else one deliberate move away.
